How to Check a Forney, TX Home for Water Damage Before Buying

Water damage hides well. A short, methodical walkthrough and the right questions can save you from an expensive surprise.

Call (844) 950-6900

Written by the Forney Water Damage Pros team. Published November 13, 2026.

Why buyers miss water damage

Sellers paint, buyers fall for staging, and hidden moisture does not announce itself on a showing. A fresh coat of paint on a ceiling can cover a stain, and a new rug can hide a damp floor. Buying a house is already stressful, so it is easy to focus on the kitchen and miss what is behind a wall.

The good news is that water leaves clues. A careful walkthrough, a moisture meter and a thorough inspection find most problems before closing. The goal is not to scare yourself away from a house, but to know what you are buying and what it may cost to fix.

What to look for on your own walkthrough

Bring a flashlight and take your time in each room.

  • Stains, ripples or fresh paint on ceilings, especially below bathrooms and around vents.
  • Warped, cupped or buckled flooring, and soft spots underfoot near sinks and tubs.
  • A musty smell in closets, garages and rooms that were closed up.
  • Cracks in drywall or brick, and doors or windows that stick.
  • Staining or rust on the water heater, under sinks and around toilets.
  • Soil or landscaping that slopes toward the foundation, and gutters that drain at the wall.

Photograph anything that looks off so you can raise it with your agent and the inspector.

Rooms and spots that deserve extra attention

Look under every sink with a flashlight. Check the cabinet floor for swelling, staining and a damp smell, and run the water to see whether the trap drips. In the garage, look at the base of the walls and the slab edge for water lines or efflorescence, which is a white powdery residue left by moisture.

In the attic, look for dark staining on the roof deck and around penetrations. In bathrooms, press gently on the floor beside the tub and toilet. A spongy feel can point to a slow leak under the finish.

These small tests take minutes. Buyers in Mesquite face the same problem, so the steps here carry over.

What the Texas seller's disclosure tells you

Texas law requires most sellers of residential property to give the buyer a written disclosure of known conditions. The form asks about items such as previous flooding, water penetration, roof leaks, previous foundation repairs and plumbing issues. A seller must answer based on what they actually know.

Read the form carefully and compare it with what you see. If it says no water penetration and you find a stain, ask about it. The form is not a substitute for an inspection, but it gives you a written record to work from.

Getting a proper inspection

Hire a licensed inspector who is willing to check moisture with a meter and look in the attic and under the house if there is access. Ask the inspector to focus on signs of past or active water intrusion. In some cases it makes sense to add a plumbing leak test or a sewer line camera inspection.

If the inspector finds a problem, ask for a specialist's opinion before closing. A slab leak or a wall that has been wet for months can change the price of a house. A written repair estimate helps you negotiate.

Questions to ask the seller or the agent

Ask if the house has ever had a leak, a flood or an insurance claim for water damage, and who did the repairs. Request any receipts and warranty paperwork for those repairs. Ask whether the roof or plumbing has been replaced and when.

You can also ask the seller to share the home's claims history report, which lists past insurance claims. If water damage was repaired, the paperwork shows how it was done. Buyers in Seagoville and other towns near Forney can ask the same questions.

What to do if you find damage

You have options. You can negotiate the price, ask the seller to repair before closing or walk away within the time allowed by your contract. Which option is right depends on how serious the problem is and how clear the cost is.

Get specific estimates from qualified professionals before you decide. A restoration company can describe the scope of drying or repair for a known water event, while a plumber can address the source. Make decisions based on documented facts, not on a rough guess.

What can change the cost of a problem you find

A defect found before closing is easier to price when you know what drives the cost.

  • Source of the waterA fixed supply line is a smaller repair than a hidden line under a slab.
  • How long it was wetLonger exposure means more removal and more drying time.
  • Materials affectedDrywall, flooring and insulation add replacement and rebuild.
  • AccessOpening walls, ceilings or a slab increases labor.
  • MoldGrowth adds containment and may bring licensing requirements.

A written estimate from a qualified contractor gives you something concrete to bring to the seller.

Reading a home's plumbing and mechanical age

Pipe material and appliance age tell you where problems are likely. Older galvanized pipes corrode from the inside, polybutylene lines have a poor reputation and water heaters usually last about a decade. Ask the inspector to note the age and condition of the water heater, the supply lines and the air conditioner drain.

A stain below a bathroom can be a leak from the tub drain or a loose fitting. A damp spot near the air handler can point to a clogged condensate line. Understanding the likely cause helps you judge whether a repair is simple or complex.

A note on flood zones and insurance

Before you commit, look up the property on the FEMA flood map and ask for a homeowners insurance quote. Insurers may ask about prior water claims and may price a policy differently for a house with a history. A flood zone designation can also mean your lender requires a separate flood policy.

Knowing this before closing avoids a surprise in the final days. If the quote or the flood designation changes your budget, you have time to adjust or negotiate.

Local note: Texas rules for sellers and builders

Section 5.008 of the Texas Property Code requires most residential sellers to give a written disclosure notice, and the Texas Real Estate Commission publishes a standard form. For newer homes, Chapter 27 of the Property Code, known as the Residential Construction Liability Act, sets out a notice and inspection process for construction defect complaints.

Forney has grown quickly, so many buyers look at newer homes. A new house can still have plumbing or grading issues, so the same walkthrough and inspection steps apply.
