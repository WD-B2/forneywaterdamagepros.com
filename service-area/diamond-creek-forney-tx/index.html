What happened in Diamond Creek in 2012

On April 3, 2012, a tornado crossed Forney and the National Weather Service survey team photographed EF-3 damage in the Diamond Creek subdivision. Its survey described the subdivision as northeast of Forney, and it also recorded EF-3 damage near the intersection of Ridgecrest and FM 548.

The Texas Tribune later noted that Diamond Creek and the other neighborhoods the storm hit had not existed a decade earlier. A local newspaper counted about $100 million in damage across the city. Crosby Elementary also lost part of its roof.

What that does and does not tell you about your house

One storm in 2012 does not tell us how any house on these streets is built, repaired or aging today. We do not claim that Diamond Creek floods or that its homes are weaker than others.

What the record does show is that wind is a real source of water damage in Forney. Rain that follows wind is how a lifted shingle or a broken window turns into a stain on a ceiling and a wet attic, and that is the case the storm water damage page is built around.

After wind opens a roof or a window

Do these in order. Safety comes before the cleanup.

  1. Check for hazards first

    Stay out of rooms with sagging ceilings, wet wiring or broken glass. Call 911 or the utility for any electrical or gas danger.

  2. Stop more rain getting in

    If it is safe and you can reach it from inside, move belongings and catch drips. Do not climb onto a wet roof.

  3. Photograph the opening and the wet rooms

    Take photos and video before cleanup. Include the roof, the ceiling and any wet floors if you can do so safely.

  4. Call the line

    Give the address, where the water is coming in and which rooms are wet. We will explain what to do next.

  5. Get the roof assessed

    A roofer closes the opening. A restoration crew dries the structure. Both are needed.

Ceilings, attics and walls after storm rain

Water from a roof opening usually soaks insulation first. Wet insulation holds moisture against the drywall, so a stain can keep growing after the rain stops. A crew checks the ceiling, the attic and the walls below with a moisture meter before deciding what to open.

The ceiling water damage page explains when a stain can be dried in place and when drywall has to come out. Wet carpet and pad go through the same decision, which the carpet page covers.

How drying works after a storm

Once standing water is out, air movers and dehumidifiers take moisture from the framing, drywall and floors. The crew reads the materials every day and moves equipment as the readings change. Drying is done when the readings match dry material, not when the surface looks dry, which is the approach the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard describes.

The EPA advises drying water-damaged areas and items within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold growth, so a call on the day of the storm helps. Read the structural drying page for the equipment and the daily steps.

Insurance, wind and flood are not the same

Rain that enters through a storm-made opening and surface water that rises into a home can be treated differently by an insurance policy. Tell your insurer exactly how the water got in, with photos of the opening.

Coverage depends on the cause and the policy terms. We do not decide coverage and we cannot promise what an insurer will approve. The Texas Department of Insurance also notes that flood normally needs its own policy.
